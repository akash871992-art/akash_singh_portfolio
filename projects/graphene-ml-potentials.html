This publication comes from my doctoral research at the University of Illinois, completed before the MS in Artificial Intelligence program. I include it to show the depth of my hands-on machine learning experience. It is separate from my course projects.

Introduction

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms. It is one of the strongest materials ever measured and conducts heat and electricity exceptionally well, which makes it attractive for electronics, sensors and lightweight composites. Designing with it requires simulating how its atoms behave under heat and stress.

The two standard options each fall short. Quantum mechanical calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) are accurate but so expensive that they are limited to a few hundred atoms. Empirical models are fast but rely on hand-fitted equations that can miss real physics. This research closes that gap with machine learning: a neural network trained on DFT results that keeps most of the accuracy at a small fraction of the cost.

Description

This work is a peer-reviewed journal article in Computational Materials Science, which I wrote as first author with my advisor, Professor Yumeng Li. It presents an artificial neural network (ANN) interatomic potential for single-layer graphene and uses it to run machine learning molecular dynamics simulations at a scale that DFT cannot reach.

How the model works

  1. Generate ground truth320 DFT calculations on 24-atom graphene cells, stretched in the armchair and zigzag directions
  2. Describe each atom30 symmetry functions per atom summarize its neighborhood within 6.5 Å
  3. Train the networkOne small network per atom, two hidden layers of 10 neurons; atomic energies add up to the total
  4. Simulate at scaleThe trained model drives molecular dynamics on sheets of 12,400 atoms
  5. Predict propertiesLattice spacing, thermal expansion, stiffness and strength from 125 K to 1,800 K

Objective

The research asked whether a neural network trained on a modest set of quantum calculations could reproduce graphene's real physical behavior reliably enough to replace DFT in large simulations. Specifically, I set out to:

  • Build an ANN potential that matches DFT energies and forces.
  • Prove it is physically reliable, not just accurate on its training data.
  • Use it to study temperature effects in systems far larger than DFT can handle, including graphene's unusual thermal expansion.

Process

  1. Data generationI sampled graphene structures from simulations of the sheet being stretched in two directions, then calculated their energies with DFT in Quantum ESPRESSO.
  2. Feature engineeringI converted each atom's surroundings into 30 Behler-type symmetry functions, which stay the same when the structure is rotated or shifted.
  3. Model design and trainingI trained a feedforward network in n2p2 on 90% of the data and held out 10% for testing, comparing two optimizers.
  4. ValidationI checked predicted energies and forces against DFT, confirmed that energy is conserved during simulation and compared phonon dispersion with experimental measurements.
  5. Large-scale simulationI ran molecular dynamics in LAMMPS on a 12,400-atom sheet across a wide temperature range, with three independent runs per temperature.
  6. Analysis and publicationI compared every predicted property with experimental, DFT and empirical results, explained the physics behind the trends and published the work after peer review.

Challenges and solutions

ChallengeHow I solved it
Each DFT calculation is expensive, so training data was limitedI sampled stretched configurations that cover the range of atomic environments the model must handle, which kept the dataset to 320 structures.
Gradient descent trained slowly, reaching only about 115 meV per atom after 400 iterationsI switched to the Levenberg–Marquardt optimizer, which reached about 5 meV per atom in fewer than 50 iterations.
Early models predicted vibration (phonon) frequencies poorlyI increased the sample size and the cutoff radius from 4.5 to 6.5 Å until the predictions matched experiment.
A low error score does not prove a model behaves physicallyI added energy-conservation tests, force-direction checks and comparisons with experimental data before using the model for predictions.

Value proposition

This research shows that I can build, train and rigorously validate a neural network on complex scientific data, and carry the work through to peer-reviewed publication.

The same discipline applies to any high-stakes model: generate trustworthy training data, choose features carefully, test against reality and be clear about the limits.

Unique value

  • End-to-end ownership. I handled every stage, from generating the data to designing the features, training the model, validating it and publishing the findings.
  • Validation beyond accuracy scores. I tested whether the model obeyed physical laws and matched experiments, not only whether its error was low. I bring that habit to every model I build.
  • Deep learning in practice. This work grounds the guidance in my decision guide (Project 3): I have built neural networks where they fit and know what they cost.

Relevance

For AI and engineering teams, the core idea is a surrogate model: a fast machine learning model trained on a slow, expensive source of truth. The same pattern speeds up simulation and decision-making across engineering, energy and finance.

For healthcare analytics, the lessons carry over directly: models are only as good as their training data, and they must be validated against real outcomes before anyone relies on them.

For materials researchers, the method scales to other two-dimensional materials. The article has been cited more than 40 times on Google Scholar.
