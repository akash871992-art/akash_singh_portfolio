Atomic edges that guide how plastics crystallize

Peer-reviewed research combining experiments, quantum calculations and molecular simulation to show how the atomic edges of a two-dimensional material steer the way polyethylene chains line up.

Introduction

Polyethylene is the most widely produced plastic in the world. How its long molecular chains pack into crystals decides how strong, stiff and transparent the final material is. Controlling that packing over long distances is difficult.

Two-dimensional materials such as molybdenum diselenide (MoSe2), a sheet only three atoms thick, offer a new way to guide it. This study asked a precise question: what role do the atomic edges of these sheets play when polyethylene crystallizes on them?

Description

This work is a peer-reviewed article in Macromolecules, a leading journal in polymer science, written by an eight-person team from the University of Illinois, Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Villanova University. I am the third author.

Key findings

  • Edges are where crystals start. Molybdenum-terminated zigzag edges of MoSe2 are preferred sites for polyethylene to begin crystallizing.
  • Chains follow the edge. Polymer chains align parallel to these edges, and the resulting crystal layers (lamellae) form arrays perpendicular to them.
  • Edge type matters. Different edge structures on the same sheet produced different crystallization, a difference the simulations trace to the atomic structure of the edge.
  • A route to long-range control. Stacked MoSe2 with parallel atomic steps could align polyethylene crystals over large areas.

Objective

The team set out to connect what happens at the scale of individual atoms to the structure of the material that forms. The goals were to:

  • Identify which edge structures of MoSe2 attract polyethylene chains most strongly.
  • Explain the observed crystal patterns with calculations and simulations.
  • Suggest a practical strategy for controlling polymer crystal alignment.

Process

  1. SynthesisThe team grew MoSe2 sheets with well-defined atomic edges.
  2. Crystallization and imagingHigh-density polyethylene was crystallized onto the sheets from a dilute solution, and the resulting structures were imaged with scanning transmission electron microscopy.
  3. Quantum calculationsAb initio calculations measured how strongly polymer segments interact with different edge structures.
  4. Molecular dynamics simulationAtomistic simulations modeled short polyethylene segments (chains of 5 and 25 carbon atoms) on MoSe2 to show how they move and align.
  5. IntegrationThe team combined experiments and simulations into one explanation and proposed edge engineering as a design strategy.

Challenges and solutions

ChallengeHow the team solved it
Real polymer chains are far too long to simulate atom by atom at useful time scalesShort chain segments of 5 and 25 carbon atoms stood in for polyethylene, keeping simulations practical while capturing the interaction with the edge.
Experiments show where crystals form but not whyQuantum calculations and molecular dynamics explained the mechanism behind what the microscope images showed.
Different methods and expertise sat at three institutionsThe team divided the work by strength, covering synthesis, imaging, calculation and simulation, and brought the results together in one study.

Tools and technologies used

Molecular dynamics simulation
Atomistic models of polymer chains interacting with MoSe2.
Ab initio (quantum) calculations
Interaction strengths between polymer segments and edge structures.
Scanning transmission electron microscopy
Atomic-resolution imaging of the edges and the polymer structures.
2D materials synthesis
Growth of MoSe2 with well-defined atomic edges.

Unique value

  • Collaboration at a national laboratory. I worked alongside scientists at Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Villanova University, coordinating results across disciplines and institutions.
  • Simulation tested against experiment. The study's conclusions rest on simulations that agree with what the microscope shows, the standard I apply to any model.
  • A complement to my graphene research. That paper shows me building a machine learning model; this one shows me using simulation as part of a larger scientific team.

Relevance

For materials and manufacturing, controlling how polymers crystallize could lead to plastics with better strength and other tailored properties, guided by engineered surfaces.

For data science and AI teams, it is an example of multi-method validation: no single source of evidence decides the answer, and simulation earns trust only when it matches real observations.

For collaborators, it shows that I can work productively in large, interdisciplinary teams. The article has been cited 15 times on Google Scholar.
