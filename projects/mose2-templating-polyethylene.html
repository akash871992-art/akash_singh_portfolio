Research / MoSe2 templating of polyethylene

Simulating how a 2D material templates plastic crystals

First-authored, peer-reviewed research using all-atom molecular dynamics to show how a single layer of MoSe2 starts and steers the crystallization of polyethylene.

This publication comes from my doctoral research at the University of Illinois, completed before the MS in Artificial Intelligence program. I include it to show my experience leading a simulation study. It is separate from my course projects.

Introduction

Adding two-dimensional materials to plastics can make composites that are stronger and more functional, but their properties depend on how the polymer chains pack next to the 2D surface. That packing happens at the scale of individual atoms and in fractions of a microsecond, which is too small and too fast for experiments to watch directly.

This study used molecular dynamics simulation to watch it happen. It builds on our earlier experimental work in Macromolecules, which showed that MoSe2 guides polyethylene crystallization, and asks how the flat surface of the material does it.

Description

This work is a 16-page peer-reviewed article in The Journal of Physical Chemistry C. I led it as first author, with collaborators at Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Villanova University and my advisor, Professor Yumeng Li.

Key findings

  • Crystals grow from the surface. Crystallization begins at the MoSe2 interface, and the crystallization front moves rapidly into the bulk polymer.
  • The surface sets the direction. At equilibrium, the chains are orientationally registered to the 2D surface, meaning the surface determines which way they point.
  • Three forces explain why. That alignment comes from a balance between the chains' conformational entropy, their adhesion to the surface and the surface's atomic-scale corrugation.
  • A design route for composites. 2D materials like MoSe2 could serve as templates for polymer nanocomposites with controlled crystal orientation.

Objective

The goal was to explain, at the atomic level, how a 2D material surface templates polymer crystallization. Specifically, the study set out to:

  • Simulate polyethylene-like chains crystallizing on a MoSe2 surface with an all-atom model.
  • Find where crystallization starts and how it spreads.
  • Identify the physical factors that decide how the chains align with the surface.

Process

  1. Choose a model systemI used n-pentacosane, a 25-carbon alkane, as a stand-in for polyethylene. It keeps the essential chemistry while staying small enough to simulate in atomic detail.
  2. Build the interfaceI constructed all-atom models of the alkane chains on a monolayer MoSe2 surface.
  3. Simulate crystallizationI ran molecular dynamics simulations in LAMMPS to follow the chains as they assembled on the surface and crystallized.
  4. Analyze order and orientationI tracked where ordered regions formed, how the crystallization front moved and how the chains oriented relative to the surface.
  5. Explain the mechanismI traced the alignment to its physical causes and connected the results to the team's earlier experimental findings.

Challenges and solutions

ChallengeHow I solved it
Full polyethylene chains are too long to simulate atom by atom at the time scales of crystallizationI used n-pentacosane as a surrogate, which keeps all-atom detail at the interface while staying computationally practical.
Crystallization at an interface is too small and fast for experiments to observe directlyThe simulations followed every atom over time, revealing where order begins and how quickly it spreads.
Alignment could come from several competing effectsI separated the roles of chain entropy, surface adhesion and surface corrugation to explain the final orientation.

Tools and technologies used

Molecular dynamics simulation
All-atom models of polymer chains on a 2D material surface.
LAMMPS
The open-source molecular dynamics engine used for the simulations.
Structural analysis
Measures of chain order, orientation and the moving crystallization front.
Statistical mechanics
Reasoning about entropy and adhesion to explain the alignment.

Value proposition

This research shows that I can lead a complex computational study from design to publication and explain what drives the results.

Designing the model, choosing sensible simplifications and tracing outcomes to their causes are the same skills I use to build analytical models that decision makers can trust.

Unique value

  • Leadership as first author. I designed and ran the simulations, analyzed the results and led the writing.
  • Mechanism, not just observation. The study explains why the chains align, separating three competing effects instead of only reporting that they align.
  • Simulation linked to experiment. It extends our team's experimental findings, showing how simulation and experiment strengthen each other.

Relevance

For materials and manufacturing, it points to 2D materials as templates for designing polymer composites with controlled structure and properties.

For data science and AI teams, it shows the value of a well-chosen surrogate. A smaller, faster model that keeps what matters can answer questions the full system cannot, an idea at the heart of machine learning surrogates such as my graphene neural network potential.

For research collaborators, it shows that I can lead a study while working with national-laboratory and university partners.
